# Business Administration — Lesson 1

## Introduction to Business Administration

**Level:** Foundation (no prior study required)
**Estimated study time:** 90 minutes reading + 45 minutes exercises
**Running case study:** a mid-sized professional football club

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

1. Define *business*, *administration* and *management*, and explain how the three relate.
2. Explain why a business exists in economic terms (value creation) and who it answers to (stakeholders).
3. Describe the four functions of management — planning, organizing, leading, controlling — and give an example of each.

6. Use a simple framework (PESTEL) to scan the environment a business operates in.

## 1. What is business administration?

A **business** is an organization that combines resources — people, money, equipment, information, time — to produce goods or services that other people are willing to pay for.

**Administration** is the work of keeping that organization running: setting direction, allocating resources, designing the rules and structures people work within, and checking that results match intentions.

Three words get used loosely and are worth separating early:

| Term | Core question it answers | Typical time horizon |
|---|---|---|
| **Administration** | *What are the rules, structures and resources?* | Long term, institutional |
| **Management** | *How do we get this done with what we have?* | Short to medium term, operational |
| **Leadership** | *Why should anyone follow this direction?* | Any horizon; about influence, not position |

In small organizations one person does all three. In large ones they separate: a board administers, executives manage, and leadership can appear at any level — including from people with no formal authority at all.

> **Note on usage.** Older textbooks (especially the classical school — Fayol, Urwick) treat administration as the senior activity and management as its execution. Modern practice and most job titles use "management" for both. Know the distinction; don't be pedantic about it in the real world.

### Why study it?

Every organization — a company, a hospital, a charity, a government agency, a football club — faces the same underlying problem: **resources are scarce and must be deployed deliberately toward a goal.** Business administration is the systematic study of how to do that well. The content transfers across sectors; only the vocabulary changes.

## 2. Why businesses exist: value creation

A business survives only if it creates **value** — if what it produces is worth more to a customer than the inputs cost to assemble.

```
Value created  =  What the customer is willing to pay  −  Cost of the resources consumed
```

Split that gap in two and you get the two ways a business can be rewarded:

- **Customer surplus** — the part of the gap the customer keeps (willingness to pay minus price). This is why they buy again.
- **Producer surplus (profit)** — the part the business keeps (price minus cost). This is what funds survival and growth.

A business that captures all the value leaves customers no reason to return. One that captures none of it cannot pay its staff. **Sustainable administration is the management of that split over time**, not the maximization of either side in a single transaction.

### Profit is not the only objective

Profit is a *constraint* and a *scorecard*, not always the purpose:

- A **cooperative** exists to serve its members; surplus is returned to them.
- A **non-profit** exists to advance a mission; surplus is reinvested and cannot be distributed.
- A **state-owned enterprise** may deliberately run at a loss to deliver a public good.
- Even a commercial firm has objectives beyond profit: market share, reputation, employee retention, risk reduction, succession.

What is universal is **solvency**: no organization of any type survives if cash out exceeds cash in indefinitely.

### Stakeholders

A **stakeholder** is anyone affected by, or able to affect, the organization.

| Stakeholder | What they typically want | What happens when ignored |
|---|---|---|
| Owners / shareholders | Return on capital, growth in value | Capital withdrawn; management replaced |
| Employees | Pay, security, development, fair treatment | Turnover, low output, industrial action |
| Customers | Quality, price, reliability, service | Revenue moves to competitors |
| Suppliers | Prompt payment, predictable orders | Worse terms, supply interruption |
| Lenders / creditors | Repayment, covenant compliance | Credit withdrawn, enforcement |
| Regulators / government | Compliance, taxes, reporting | Fines, licence loss, prosecution |
| Community / public | Employment, responsible conduct | Reputational damage, opposition |

Stakeholder interests **conflict routinely** — higher wages reduce short-run profit; lower prices please customers and squeeze suppliers. A large part of the administrator's job is making defensible trade-offs among them and being able to explain the reasoning afterwards.

## 3. The four functions of management

The standard model, traceable to Henri Fayol (1916) and still the backbone of the discipline, is **P-O-L-C**.

### 3.1 Planning — deciding what to do

Setting objectives and choosing the route to them. It operates at three levels:

| Level | Horizon | Who | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Strategic** | 3–10 years | Board / executives | "Become the leading supplier in West Africa" |
| **Tactical** | 1–3 years | Department heads | "Open two regional depots and hire 40 staff" |
| **Operational** | Days to months | Supervisors | "Fulfil 200 orders this week with 99% accuracy" |

Good objectives are **SMART**: Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound. "Improve customer service" is not an objective. "Cut average response time from 48 hours to 12 hours by 31 March" is.

### 3.2 Organizing — arranging resources to match the plan

Deciding who does what, who reports to whom, and how work is grouped and coordinated. Key concepts:

- **Division of labour** — splitting work into specialized tasks, which raises skill and speed but increases the need for coordination.
- **Authority** — the right to make decisions and commit resources.
- **Responsibility** — the obligation to perform. *Authority can be delegated; responsibility cannot.* A manager who delegates a task still answers for the outcome.
- **Accountability** — being answerable for results.
- **Span of control** — how many people report to one manager. Wide spans flatten the organization and demand more autonomy; narrow spans allow closer supervision and create more hierarchy.
- **Centralization vs decentralization** — whether decisions are concentrated at the top or pushed to where the information is. Decentralize when local knowledge matters and speed is valuable; centralize when consistency, scale or control matters more.

### 3.3 Leading — getting people to perform

Direction, motivation, communication and conflict resolution. Two foundational ideas:

- **Maslow's hierarchy of needs** — people are motivated by unmet needs in rough order: physiological → safety → belonging → esteem → self-actualization. Practical takeaway: a recognition programme will not compensate for wages people cannot live on.
- **Herzberg's two-factor theory** — *hygiene factors* (pay, conditions, supervision, job security) cause dissatisfaction when poor but do not motivate when good; *motivators* (achievement, recognition, responsibility, growth) are what actually drive engagement. Practical takeaway: fix hygiene first, then build motivation — they are different levers, not one dial.

### 3.4 Controlling — checking and correcting

The feedback loop that makes the other three functions self-correcting:

```
Set standards  →  Measure actual performance  →  Compare against standard
      ↑                                                     │
      └──────────  Correct the deviation (or the standard) ──┘
```

Control mechanisms include budgets and variance analysis, KPIs and dashboards, quality inspection, internal audit, and performance appraisal.

**Two failure modes to recognize:**

- **Too little control** — problems surface only when they are expensive; no one is accountable.
- **Too much control** — people optimize for the measure instead of the goal (*Goodhart's law*: "when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure"). A call centre rewarded purely on call volume will hang up on customers.

## 4. The functional areas of an organization

Most organizations, whatever their size, contain the same set of functions. In a five-person firm one person wears several hats; in a large one each is a department.

| Function | Accountable for | Typical measures |
|---|---|---|
| **Operations / Production** | Delivering the product or service | Output, unit cost, defect rate, on-time delivery |
| **Marketing & Sales** | Understanding demand and converting it to revenue | Market share, cost per acquisition, conversion rate |
| **Finance & Accounting** | Funding, recording, reporting, controlling money | Margin, cash flow, return on capital, budget variance |
| **Human Resources** | Recruiting, developing, retaining and paying people | Turnover, time-to-hire, absenteeism, engagement |
| **Information Technology** | Systems, data and their security | Uptime, incident rate, project delivery |
| **Legal & Compliance** | Contracts, regulation, risk | Disputes, breaches, audit findings |
| **Administration / Office Management** | Records, facilities, procurement, coordination | Cost per head, turnaround time, service levels |

Two structural points worth carrying forward:

1. **Functions optimize locally by default.** Sales wants generous credit terms; Finance wants fast payment; Operations wants long, uninterrupted production runs; Marketing wants variety. Cross-functional conflict is structural, not personal — it is designed in, and coordination is the administrator's job.
2. **The customer experiences the process, not the department.** Value flows *horizontally* across functions while authority runs *vertically* through them. Most service failures happen at the hand-offs.

## 5. Forms of business ownership

The legal form determines who owns the business, who is liable for its debts, how it is taxed, and how easily it can raise money.

| Form | Owners | Liability | Taxation | Continuity | Best suited to |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Sole proprietorship** | One | **Unlimited** — personal assets at risk | Owner's personal income | Ends with the owner | Small, low-risk, owner-operated trade |
| **Partnership (general)** | 2+ | **Unlimited, joint and several** | Partners individually | Dissolves on partner exit unless agreed | Professional practices, small ventures |
| **Limited partnership** | General + limited partners | Limited partners risk only their stake | Partners individually | More stable | Investment vehicles, funds |
| **Limited liability company** (LLC / Ltd / GmbH) | Members / shareholders | **Limited to capital contributed** | Entity or pass-through, by jurisdiction | Perpetual | Most SMEs |
| **Public corporation** (PLC / Inc.) | Public shareholders | Limited | Corporate tax; dividends taxed again | Perpetual | Large, capital-hungry firms |
| **Cooperative** | Members (one member, one vote) | Usually limited | Varies; surplus often distributed as patronage | Perpetual | Member-service organizations |
| **Non-profit / NGO** | No owners; trustees govern | Limited | Often exempt on mission income | Perpetual | Mission-driven bodies |

### The trade-off in one sentence

**Simplicity and control at one end, limited liability and access to capital at the other.** A sole proprietor keeps every decision and every profit — and every debt. A public company can raise hundreds of millions — and must answer to shareholders, auditors, a regulator and the financial press.

### Why limited liability matters so much

**Limited liability** means the owners' loss is capped at what they invested; creditors cannot pursue their homes or savings. This single legal innovation is what makes large-scale, risky, long-horizon investment possible — most people will not fund a venture that could bankrupt them personally. The trade-off society demands in return is **disclosure**: registered accounts, filings, and audit.

## 6. The business environment

No organization is self-contained. Administrators must read the environment they operate in and adapt to it.

**Internal environment** — what you control: people, culture, capital, facilities, systems, brand, intellectual property.

**Micro (task) environment** — outside the firm but directly interacting with it: customers, competitors, suppliers, distributors, lenders, unions. You influence these but do not control them.

**Macro environment** — broad forces you must adapt to. Scan them with **PESTEL**:

| Factor | What to look at | Example impact |
|---|---|---|
| **P**olitical | Stability, policy, trade, taxation | New import tariff raises input cost 12% |
| **E**conomic | Growth, inflation, interest rates, FX, employment | Rate rise makes expansion borrowing unaffordable |
| **S**ocial | Demographics, values, lifestyle, education | Ageing population shifts demand to services |
| **T**echnological | Automation, platforms, AI, R&D | Self-service channel removes a whole cost layer |
| **E**nvironmental | Climate, resources, sustainability rules | Emissions reporting becomes mandatory |
| **L**egal | Employment, consumer, competition, data law | Data protection rules force a system rebuild |

**How to use PESTEL properly:** the point is not to list factors, it is to identify the two or three that would *change a decision*, estimate their likelihood and impact, and assign someone to monitor them. A PESTEL table with twenty generic bullet points and no conclusion is an exercise, not analysis.

## 7. Case study: a football club as a business

Take a mid-table professional club. It is an unusually clear teaching case because its sporting objective and its financial objective genuinely pull against each other.

**Value creation.** The product is entertainment and identity. Customers include matchday attendees, broadcasters, sponsors and merchandise buyers. Note that the highest-paying customer — the broadcaster — is not the one in the stadium, which shapes everything from kick-off times to ticket pricing.

**Stakeholder conflict.** Supporters want trophies and cheap tickets. Owners want the asset to appreciate. Players and agents want wages. The league regulator wants financial sustainability rules obeyed. A single decision — spend heavily on a striker — pleases three of these and alarms two. There is no option that satisfies everyone; the administrator's job is to choose and justify.

**The four functions.**

- *Planning* — strategic: "establish the club in the top division and build a self-funding academy within five years." Tactical: the season's transfer budget and wage ceiling. Operational: this week's matchday staffing.
- *Organizing* — the split between the **football side** (manager, coaches, scouts, medical, academy) and the **business side** (commercial, ticketing, finance, facilities, media). Who controls transfer spending — the manager or a sporting director reporting to the board — is a genuine organizing decision with real consequences.
- *Leading* — the manager motivating a squad is the visible version; the chief executive holding staff through a relegation fight is the harder one.
- *Controlling* — wage-to-revenue ratio (the single most-watched control metric in the sport), squad fitness and injury rates, season-ticket renewals, compliance with financial regulations.

**Ownership form.** Clubs run the full range: privately held companies, publicly listed ones, and **member-owned associations** where supporters elect the president. The ownership form changes who the club is *for* — a supporter-owned club cannot easily relocate or raise ticket prices sharply, because the customers are the voters.

**PESTEL in one line each.** *Political:* stadium safety and public-order regulation. *Economic:* a recession hits discretionary spending and season-ticket renewals first. *Social:* growth of the women's game; global rather than local fanbases. *Technological:* streaming, data analytics, wearable tracking. *Environmental:* travel emissions and sustainability reporting. *Legal:* employment law for players, financial fair play, broadcasting rights.

## 8. Key terms

**Administration** — setting the structures, rules and resource allocations an organization runs on.
**Accountability** — being answerable for a result.
**Authority** — the right to decide and to commit resources.
**Centralization** — concentration of decision-making at senior levels.
**Controlling** — measuring performance against a standard and correcting deviations.
**Delegation** — passing authority for a task down the hierarchy while retaining responsibility.
**Division of labour** — splitting work into specialized tasks.
**Limited liability** — owners' losses are capped at their investment.
**Management** — achieving objectives through the coordinated effort of others.
**Organizing** — arranging people and resources to carry out the plan.
**PESTEL** — a scan of political, economic, social, technological, environmental and legal forces.
**Planning** — setting objectives and choosing how to reach them.
**Span of control** — number of subordinates reporting to one manager.
**Stakeholder** — anyone affected by, or able to affect, the organization.
**SMART objective** — specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, time-bound.
**Value creation** — the gap between what customers will pay and what resources cost.

## 9. Review questions

Answer in your own words before checking the key.

1. Distinguish administration, management and leadership. Give one example of each in a single organization.
2. "The purpose of a business is to make a profit." Assess this statement.
3. List the four functions of management and state one activity under each for a school.
4. A manager delegates a report to an assistant, who misses the deadline. Who is responsible, and why?
5. Explain two consequences of widening a manager's span of control from 5 to 15.
6. A founder plans to open a delivery service with two vehicles and a partner. Recommend a legal form and justify it against two alternatives.
7. Explain limited liability and why society demands disclosure in exchange for it.
8. Identify one factor from each PESTEL category affecting a supermarket chain in your country.
9. Give a real example of Goodhart's law and describe a control that would avoid it.
10. Why do conflicts between Sales and Finance recur even when both departments are well run?

### Answer key

1. **Administration** sets structures and allocates resources (the board approves next year's budget); **management** executes within them (a department head schedules the work to hit that budget); **leadership** influences people toward a direction (a senior engineer with no direct reports persuades the team to adopt a new standard). The same person may do all three in different moments.

2. Partially true and incomplete. Profit is necessary for a commercial firm's survival, funds reinvestment and signals that value is being created. But it is better understood as a *constraint and scorecard* than the purpose: cooperatives, non-profits and state enterprises pursue other ends, and even commercial firms hold objectives — reputation, retention, risk, succession — that a single-minded focus on profit would damage. **Solvency**, not profit, is the truly universal requirement.

3. *Planning:* setting the academic targets for the year. *Organizing:* assigning teachers to classes and building the timetable. *Leading:* a head of department motivating staff through exam season. *Controlling:* reviewing results against targets and adjusting teaching plans.

4. **The manager.** Authority for the task was delegated; responsibility for the outcome was not and cannot be. The manager is answerable to their own superior and should have built in a checkpoint before the deadline. The assistant is accountable *to the manager*, which is a separate relationship.

5. (i) The organization flattens — a layer of management may become unnecessary, cutting cost and shortening communication chains. (ii) Supervision per person falls sharply, so the manager must rely on standardized procedures, clear objectives and self-managing staff; if the work is complex or the staff inexperienced, quality and development suffer.

6. A **limited liability company** is the strongest recommendation. Against a *sole proprietorship*: there are two founders, so a proprietorship does not fit, and vehicle operation carries genuine accident liability that would otherwise reach personal assets. Against a *general partnership*: a partnership is simpler and cheaper to form but exposes both founders to unlimited joint and several liability — in a business whose main risk is third-party injury claims, that exposure is the deciding factor. The LLC also makes it easier to admit an investor or transfer a stake later.

7. Limited liability caps an owner's loss at the capital they contributed, so creditors cannot pursue personal assets. It makes large-scale and risky investment possible, since few would fund ventures that could bankrupt them personally. Because creditors and the public now bear risk the owners have shed, the law requires **disclosure** — registration, filed accounts, audit — so those dealing with the company can assess it.

8. Sample: *Political* — food-safety and labelling regulation. *Economic* — food inflation squeezing basket size. *Social* — shift toward smaller, more frequent shops and plant-based products. *Technological* — self-checkout and online ordering. *Environmental* — packaging and food-waste rules. *Legal* — employment and minimum-wage law. (Adapt to your own country and defend each with a specific effect.)

9. Example: a hospital emergency department targeted on "patients seen within four hours" discharges or admits patients just before the deadline regardless of clinical need. A better control uses a **balanced set** of measures — waiting time *plus* readmission rate, clinical outcome and patient feedback — so gaming one measure shows up in another.

10. Because their objectives are structurally opposed by design, not by attitude. Sales is measured on revenue and therefore favours generous credit terms and discounts; Finance is measured on cash flow and bad-debt losses and therefore resists them. Each is behaving correctly within its own mandate. The conflict is resolved not by blaming either department but by **coordination at a higher level** — a shared credit policy, joint targets, or a measure that both are accountable to.

## 10. Practical exercise

Choose a real organization you know well — your employer, a local business, a school, or a football club.

1. Write a one-paragraph description of the value it creates and for whom.
2. List its six most important stakeholders and one thing each wants. Mark one pair whose interests conflict and say how the organization currently handles it.
3. Map its functional areas. Note any that are missing or combined, and say whether that is a sensible choice at its size.
4. Identify its legal form. State one advantage and one disadvantage that form gives it.
5. Build a PESTEL table, then **discard everything except the three factors most likely to change a decision in the next two years.** Explain each in two sentences.
6. Find one control mechanism it uses and assess whether it is at risk of Goodhart's law.

Target length: 800–1,200 words. Most of the marks are in steps 2, 5 and 6 — the ones that require judgement rather than recall.
