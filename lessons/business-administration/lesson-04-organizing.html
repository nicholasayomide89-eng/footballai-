Unit 4 · The organizing function

Organizing and Organizational Structure

Once a plan exists, somebody has to decide who does what, who answers to whom, and where decisions get made. That is organizing — the second function of management, and the one that quietly determines whether a good plan ever gets executed.

4.1

What organizing actually means

2 min

Organizing is the process of arranging and structuring work so that an organization's goals can be achieved. It sits second in the classical sequence of management functions — planning, organizing, leading, controlling — because you cannot allocate work until you know what the work is for.

Organizing produces three visible outputs:

  • Organizational structure — the formal arrangement of jobs, reporting lines and groupings.
  • The organizational chart — the diagram of that structure. Note that the chart is a picture of the structure, not the structure itself.
  • Job design — the specific duties, authority and relationships attached to each position.

The whole exercise is often summed up as organizational design: making deliberate decisions about six building blocks, which are the subject of the next part.

4.2

The six building blocks of structure

4 min
  1. Work specialization (division of labour). Dividing activities into separate jobs so each worker performs one part. Adam Smith's pin factory showed the productivity gain; modern practice recognises the limit, where boredom, fatigue and absenteeism cancel out the efficiency.
  2. Departmentalization. Grouping the specialized jobs back together. Five common bases: functional (marketing, finance, production), product, geographic, process (stages of a workflow) and customer (retail, wholesale, government accounts). Cross-functional teams cut across these groupings.
  3. Chain of command. The unbroken line of authority from the top of the organization to the lowest level, clarifying who reports to whom. It carries three ideas: authority (the right to give orders), responsibility (the obligation to perform) and unity of command — Fayol's rule that each employee should report to one and only one superior.
  4. Span of control. The number of employees a manager can effectively supervise. Wide spans → fewer levels → a flat structure, cheaper but demanding of the manager. Narrow spans → many levels → a tall structure, with tighter supervision but higher administrative cost and slower vertical communication.
  5. Centralization / decentralization. How far decision-making authority sits at the top. Centralized: top managers decide with little input from below. Decentralized: lower-level managers who are closer to the action decide. Employee empowerment is decentralization pushed to the individual job.
  6. Formalization. The degree to which jobs are standardized by written rules, procedures and job descriptions. High formalization means little discretion; low formalization means employees exercise judgement.
Exam point Both diagrams contain seven positions. Only the span of control differs — and that single decision changes the number of levels, the wage bill, the speed of communication and how much discretion each worker gets.
4.3

Authority, responsibility and delegation

2 min

Authority attaches to the position, not the person — it is the right inherent in a managerial post to give orders and expect them to be obeyed. Three types appear in most structures:

  • Line authority — a manager's right to direct the work of subordinates in the chain of command (production manager over machine operators).
  • Staff authority — advisory and supportive. Human resources, legal and quality specialists assist and advise line managers but do not command them.
  • Functional authority — a limited right for a specialist to direct others outside their own chain on a specific matter, such as the finance department enforcing expenditure procedures across all units.

Delegation is the downward assignment of authority to another person to carry out specific duties. It normally moves in three steps: assign the task, grant the authority needed to do it, and create obligation (responsibility) for its completion.

The rule that is always tested A manager can delegate authority, but never accountability. The delegating manager remains answerable to their own superior for the result. Equally, authority should match responsibility — giving someone a duty without the authority to discharge it is the classic delegation failure.
4.4

Common organizational designs

2 min
DesignDefining featureStrength / weakness
SimpleLow departmentalization, wide spans, authority centralized in one person, little formalization.Fast and cheap; risky as the business grows and one owner becomes the bottleneck.
FunctionalSimilar specialities grouped together — all marketing in one unit, all production in another.Economies of scale and deep expertise; narrow functional silos that lose sight of the whole.
DivisionalSeparate, largely autonomous units by product, market or region; division heads run their own operations.Focus on results and accountability; duplication of activities and resources across divisions.
MatrixSpecialists from functional departments assigned to projects, reporting to a functional manager and a project manager.Flexible use of expertise; dual reporting breaks unity of command and breeds conflict over priorities.
Team-basedThe entire organization is made of work teams with little hierarchy.Empowerment and speed; needs mature, trained members and clear accountability.
Network / virtualA small core of staff outsources major business functions to outside suppliers.Very low overheads and high flexibility; reduced control over work performed by others.
4.5

Choosing a structure: mechanistic or organic

1 min

There is no universally best structure. Two extremes anchor the choice. A mechanistic organization is rigid and tightly controlled — high specialization, rigid departmentalization, narrow spans, high formalization, centralized decisions. An organic organization is highly adaptive and loose — cross-functional teams, wide spans, low formalization, decentralized decisions and free-flowing information.

Which one fits is a contingency question, decided by four factors:

  • Strategy — structure follows strategy (Chandler). An innovation strategy needs organic flexibility; a cost-minimization strategy favours mechanistic tightness.
  • Size — large organizations drift towards specialization, rules and mechanistic form.
  • Technology — routine, mass-production technology suits mechanistic design; non-routine, unit or custom work suits organic design (Woodward).
  • Environmental uncertainty — stable and simple environments tolerate mechanistic structures; dynamic, uncertain environments demand organic ones (Burns and Stalker).
4.6

Formal and informal organization

1 min

The formal organization is the deliberate structure shown on the chart: official positions, prescribed relationships, sanctioned communication channels. The informal organization is the natural network of social relationships that arises spontaneously as people work together — friendship groups, opinion leaders with no title, and the grapevine along which information travels faster than any memo.

A competent administrator does not try to abolish the informal organization; it is inevitable, it satisfies social needs, and it often gets urgent work done across the gaps a chart leaves open. The task is to keep it aligned with formal objectives rather than working against them.
