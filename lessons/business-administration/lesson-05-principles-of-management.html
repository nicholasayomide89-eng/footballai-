    Principles of Management: Planning, Organizing, Leading and Controlling

    Every organization — a government office, a hamlet office, a small business, a non-profit — needs someone to decide what work has to be done, who will do it, how people will be guided, and whether the work was done properly. That is management, and it is the same four-part job whether you supervise two people or two hundred.

    5.1

    Introduction: what management means

    6 min

    Management is the work of getting an organization's goals accomplished through other people and with the resources the organization has. Those resources are usually people, money, time, equipment, supplies and information. A manager does not do all the work personally. A manager makes sure the right work gets done, by the right people, at the right time, at an acceptable cost and to an acceptable standard.

    The word sounds formal, but the job is practical. Consider a very ordinary situation.

    Workplace example

    A supervisor in an Iqaluit organization is responsible for five employees. The supervisor must determine what work needs to be completed, assign responsibilities, communicate expectations, solve problems, and check whether the work was completed correctly.

    Notice that the supervisor's day contains four different kinds of thinking, and they always appear in that order:

    The supervisor is…Management functionThe question being answered
    Deciding what work needs to be completedPlanningWhat are we trying to achieve, and by when?
    Assigning responsibilitiesOrganizingWho does what, with which resources?
    Communicating expectations and solving problemsLeadingHow do I guide and support the people doing the work?
    Checking whether the work was done correctlyControllingDid we get the result we planned for?

    These are the four functions of management. They were first described in the early 1900s by Henri Fayol, a French mining executive, and they are still the standard way management is taught because they describe what managers actually do in almost any organization.

    Manager, supervisor, employee — what is the difference?

    The titles vary between workplaces, but the responsibilities are usually layered like this:

    • Manager or director. Responsible for a whole unit, department or organization. Sets goals, approves budgets and spending, decides on staffing, and answers to senior management, an owner or a board.
    • Supervisor. Responsible for a small team and for the day-to-day work. Assigns tasks, answers questions, approves timesheets and leave, checks quality, and reports problems upward. Most people's first management job is a supervisor's job.
    • Employee. Responsible for completing assigned work correctly, on time, and according to the organization's procedures, and for telling the supervisor when something cannot be done.

    The responsibilities a supervisor carries

    WorkDeciding priorities, assigning tasks, setting deadlines and making sure the workload is realistic.
    PeopleTraining, answering questions, giving feedback, handling conflict, approving leave and scheduling coverage.
    MoneyStaying inside the approved budget, reviewing purchase orders and invoices, and explaining differences.
    InformationKeeping records, reporting to senior management, and passing information down to staff accurately.
    StandardsApplying the organization's policies and procedures consistently to everyone, including yourself.
    AccountabilityAnswering for the unit's results — the good ones and the poor ones — and correcting what went wrong.
    Key idea

    A supervisor can delegate a task. A supervisor cannot delegate accountability. If you assign the month-end invoices to an employee and they are not entered, the employee is responsible for the task, but you still have to answer for the unit's month-end.

    5.2

    Planning

    8 min

    Planning means deciding in advance what has to be achieved and how it will be achieved. It is the first function because every other function depends on it: you cannot assign work, guide people or check results until you know what the work is supposed to produce.

    Planning is made up of seven practical activities.

    1. Setting goalsDeciding the result you want, in words specific enough that anyone can tell whether it happened. “Improve filing” is not a goal. “All 2025 client files transferred to the new retention schedule by March 31” is.
    2. Identifying tasksBreaking the goal into the individual jobs that produce it, including the small ones people forget — approvals, signatures, shipping time.
    3. Creating deadlinesGiving every task a date. Work backwards from the final deadline so that each step has the time it needs.
    4. Preparing budgetsEstimating what the work will cost and confirming the money is available and approved before it is committed.
    5. Identifying resourcesNaming the people, hours, equipment, software, supplies and approvals the plan needs. A plan that assumes a person who is on leave is not a plan.
    6. Anticipating problemsAsking in advance what is most likely to go wrong, and how much damage it would do.
    7. Developing backup plansDeciding now what you will do if the likely problem happens, so the decision is not made in a panic later.

    Why northern planning is less forgiving

    In many southern workplaces, a forgotten order is a two-day problem — you re-order, you pay for faster shipping, the work continues. In Nunavut, the same mistake can be a one-year problem. Sealift runs on a short seasonal schedule, air freight costs far more per kilogram and is limited by weight and space, and weather can delay both. Planning ahead is not an administrative nicety in the North; it is the difference between having what you need and doing without it.

    Nunavut example — ordering before the final sealift

    A Government of Nunavut office needs to order supplies before the final sealift of the season. Before anything is ordered, the manager has to work through six questions.

    The manager must determine…Planning activityWhat it looks like in practice
    What supplies are requiredIdentifying tasks and resourcesCounting what is on hand, estimating a year of use, and asking each team what they will run out of.
    How much they will costPreparing a budgetPricing the order, adding freight and taxes, and comparing the total with the funds remaining in the supply budget.
    When they must be orderedCreating deadlinesWorking backwards from the shipping cut-off date, allowing time for quotes, approval and the vendor to pack the order.
    Who will prepare the purchase orderIdentifying resourcesNaming the employee who will do it, confirming they have the training and system access, and telling them the deadline.
    Whether the expenditure is within the approved budgetBudgeting and approvalChecking the budget line before committing, and obtaining the spending approval the organization's policy requires.
    What could happen if the shipment misses the sealiftAnticipating problems and backup plansAir freight for essentials only, a reduced order, borrowing from another office, or changing how the supplies are used for the year.
    Common mistake

    Treating the order date as the deadline. The real deadline is the vendor's shipping cut-off, and the office deadline has to be earlier than that — early enough to cover quotes, approval signatures and the time the vendor needs to assemble and deliver the order to the shipper.

    Think like a manager

    The question. You are the manager of that office. The supply order is due to the vendor in three weeks, and the employee who normally prepares purchase orders has just been approved for four weeks of leave starting Monday. What do you do first, and which planning activity are you using?

    5.3

    Organizing

    8 min

    Organizing means arranging people, work and resources so the plan can actually be carried out. Planning decides what; organizing decides who, with what, and in what order.

    • Assigning work. Matching each task to the employee whose job, training and system access fit it — and saying clearly who owns it. Work that is assigned to “the team” is often done by nobody.
    • Delegating responsibilities. Giving an employee a task and the authority to complete it — the approvals, the access, the ability to make the small decisions the task requires. Delegating a job without the authority to do it sets the employee up to fail.
    • Organizing employees. Deciding who works on what, who covers the front counter, who backs up whom, and who answers to whom while you are away.
    • Managing resources. Making sure the money, equipment, software licences, supplies and workspace the tasks need are available when they are needed.
    • Establishing procedures. Writing down the routine steps — how an invoice is processed, how a complaint is logged — so the work is done the same way by anyone.
    • Managing schedules. Building work schedules and leave coverage so that essential services stay covered, including during holidays, training and sick days.
    • Maintaining records. Keeping files, approvals, timesheets and correspondence complete, accurate, findable and stored for as long as the organization's retention rules require.
    Key idea — delegation

    Delegation has three parts: the task (what must be done), the authority (the permission and access to do it), and the accountability (which stays with the supervisor). A supervisor who keeps every task also becomes the bottleneck for the whole unit — and the unit stops the moment that supervisor is away.

    5.4

    Leading

    7 min

    Leading means working with and through people so that the plan gets carried out well. Planning and organizing deal with work; leading deals with the people doing the work. It is the function that separates a supervisor who is merely organized from one people will actually work for.

    • Giving clear instructions. Saying what the result should look like, when it is due, and what “done properly” means. A useful test: ask the employee to tell you back what they are going to do.
    • Communicating with employees. Passing on decisions, changes and reasons, and listening to what staff tell you about the work. Most workplace problems that reach a manager began as information that did not travel.
    • Motivating employees. Recognizing good work specifically and publicly, explaining why a task matters, giving people work that builds their skills, and being consistent about fairness.
    • Handling disagreements. Dealing with conflict early, privately and factually, before it becomes a formal complaint or a silent stand-off that slows the whole office.
    • Giving feedback. Telling people how they are doing while it can still make a difference. Feedback works when it is specific, timely, about the work rather than the person, and followed by a clear expectation.
    • Supporting teamwork. Making sure people help each other, share information, and understand how their work connects to everyone else's.
    • Making decisions. Deciding — and saying so — when the staff cannot resolve something themselves. An unmade decision is itself a decision, usually a bad one.

    5.5

    Controlling

    8 min

    Controlling means checking whether the actual results match the plan, and correcting the difference when they do not. It is not about controlling people. It is about comparing what happened with what was supposed to happen.

    Controlling always follows the same three steps: measure what actually happened, compare it with the standard or the plan, and act on the difference — either correct the work, or change the plan because the plan was wrong.

    • Reviewing completed work. Looking at the finished product before it goes out, not after a client, resident or senior manager finds the error.
    • Comparing results with goals. Asking whether what was produced is what the plan asked for — in quantity, quality and timing.
    • Checking budgets. Comparing actual spending with the approved budget regularly, so that a problem appears as a small difference in month three rather than a deficit in month twelve.
    • Reviewing deadlines. Checking progress before a due date, while there is still time to add help or renegotiate the date.
    • Correcting errors. Fixing the specific mistake, then asking why it happened, so the cause is corrected rather than only the symptom.
    • Monitoring performance. Following how individuals and the unit are doing over time, with notes kept as they happen rather than reconstructed at review time.
    • Following policies and procedures. Applying the organization's rules consistently — including the ones about approvals, conflict of interest and record keeping.

    Finance example: the invoice that does not match

    Finance scenario

    A department approved a purchase requisition for $1,105.00 before tax. The goods arrived and the invoice has now reached your desk. The invoice subtotal is $1,305.00 before tax — $200.00 more than the approved amount. Everything else on the invoice looks normal: correct vendor, correct department, correct address.

    Should the invoice be paid immediately?

    Why this matters

    A $200 difference is small enough to be tempting to ignore and large enough to matter. It is also a control point: the requisition is the approved amount, the invoice is the claimed amount, and the receiving record is the proof of what actually arrived. When all three agree, payment is routine. When they do not, the difference has to be explained before payment, because after payment the money is gone and the leverage with the vendor is gone with it.

    5.6

    The four functions working together

    5 min

    The four functions are usually drawn as a list, but they work as a cycle. What you learn while controlling changes the next plan. A manager who never closes that loop keeps making the same mistake every year.

    Select each stage to see what it means and an example from the sealift supply order.

    Key idea

    The functions overlap in real time. On any given morning a supervisor is planning next month, organizing today, leading a conversation and controlling last week's numbers — often within the same hour. The cycle describes the logic of the work, not a timetable.

    Case study

    You are an administrative supervisor for a small organization in Iqaluit. It is Monday morning, 8:30 a.m. You have four employees. You open your email and find all of the following waiting for you at once:

    • Three invoices need approval before month-end, which is Wednesday.
    • An employee has called in sick and will be out today.
    • A resident has complained about a missed service and is expecting a response.
    • Office supplies must be ordered.
    • A report for senior management is due tomorrow.
    • Two employees are asking for your help with competing priorities — each has been told their work comes first.

      The assignment

      Scenario

      You are the supervisor of an administrative office with four employees. Your department has several assignments that must be completed this week: month-end financial paperwork, a supply order, a report for senior management, and the regular front-counter service that residents depend on every day. One employee is new and still learning the procedures. Another has more work assigned than the week will hold.

      Your task. In approximately 300 to 500 words, explain how you would use each of the four management functions to get the week's work done. Deal with all four, and use the scenario — a general description of the functions without the office in it will not earn full marks.

      1. Planning — what has to be achieved this week, in what order, by when, and what could go wrong.
      2. Organizing — who does what, what you delegate, and how you handle the new employee and the overloaded one.
      3. Leading — what you communicate, how you motivate and support the team, and how you handle any disagreement.
      4. Controlling — what you check during the week, how you know the work met the standard, and what you do if it did not.

        Business Administration · Lesson 5

        Principles of Management — Study Sheet

        Planning · Organizing · Leading · Controlling

        1. Key definitions

        Management
        Getting an organization's goals accomplished through people and resources.
        Planning
        Deciding in advance what must be achieved and how: goals, tasks, deadlines, budgets, resources, risks, backup plans.
        Organizing
        Arranging people, work and resources so the plan can be carried out: assigning, delegating, scheduling, procedures, records.
        Leading
        Guiding people so the work gets done well: instructions, communication, motivation, feedback, conflict, decisions.
        Controlling
        Measuring actual results, comparing them with the plan, and correcting the difference.
        Delegation
        Assigning a task and the authority to complete it. Accountability stays with the supervisor.
        Accountability
        Answering for results, including work you assigned to someone else.
        Policy
        A rule that states what the organization requires or permits.
        Procedure
        The step-by-step method for carrying a policy out.
        Budget
        A plan for money: what may be spent, on what, over what period.

        2. The four functions at a glance

        FunctionQuestion it answersTypical activities
        PlanningWhat are we trying to achieve, and by when?Goals, task lists, deadlines, budgets, resources, risks, backup plans
        OrganizingWho does what, with what?Assigning, delegating, schedules, coverage, procedures, records
        LeadingHow do I guide the people doing the work?Instructions, communication, motivation, feedback, conflict, decisions
        ControllingDid we get the result we planned for?Reviews, budget checks, deadline checks, corrections, monitoring

        3. Workplace examples

        • Planning — sealift supply order. Determine what supplies are required, what they cost, when they must be ordered, who prepares the purchase order, whether the cost is within budget, and what happens if the shipment misses the sealift.
        • Organizing — an absence. One employee is sick; front-counter coverage and the month-end invoices are both due. Reassign the counter, move the lowest-deadline task, tell everyone what changed.
        • Leading — a disputed task. Two employees each believe the other owns tomorrow's report. Speak to them together, establish the facts, assign the work clearly in writing, address the assignment breakdown afterwards.
        • Controlling — an invoice difference. Requisition approved at $1,105 before tax; invoice subtotal arrives at $1,305. Hold payment, investigate the $200 difference against the purchase order and receiving record, then approve, amend or return the invoice.

        4. Important points to remember

        • The functions run in a cycle: plan → organize → lead → control → improve the plan.
        • You can delegate a task. You cannot delegate accountability.
        • Delegation without authority is not delegation; it is a trap.
        • A goal that cannot be measured cannot be controlled.
        • Controlling means comparing results with the plan — not controlling people.
        • Check progress before the deadline, while there is still time to act.
        • Northern logistics shorten the margin for error: sealift cut-offs and air freight costs make late planning expensive.
        • Document decisions, approvals and conversations as they happen.

        5. Common mistakes

        • Assigning work to “the team” instead of to a named person with a date.
        • Treating the internal order date, rather than the shipping cut-off, as the real deadline.
        • Approving an invoice that does not match the approved requisition because the difference looks small.
        • Taking sides in a staff disagreement before establishing the facts.
        • Giving feedback only at performance review time, when it is too late to change the result.
        • Doing the work yourself because it is faster than explaining it — which guarantees it will be faster next time too.
        • Confusing being busy with managing. Answering email all day is not planning, organizing, leading or controlling.

        6. Exam review questions

        1. Define management in one sentence and name the four functions in order.
        2. List five activities that belong to planning.
        3. Explain the difference between a policy and a procedure, with an example of each.
        4. What three things must a supervisor hand over for delegation to work?
        5. Why does accountability stay with the supervisor after a task is delegated?
        6. Describe the three steps of the controlling process.
        7. An invoice subtotal is $200 higher than the approved requisition. State what you would do and why.
        8. Two employees disagree about who owns a task due tomorrow. What do you do first, and which function is it?
        9. Give one Nunavut workplace example for each of the four functions.
        10. Explain why communication, organization and accountability matter for a supervisor. Use an example.
