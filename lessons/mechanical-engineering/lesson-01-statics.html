Forces, Moments and Static Equilibrium

Every machine part, bracket and beam a mechanical engineer designs must first be shown not to move. Statics is the arithmetic of things that stay still — and it is the gateway to stress analysis, machine design and everything that follows.

1.1

The language: quantities, units, idealizations

2 min

Engineering mechanics is the study of what forces do to bodies. It splits in two: statics, where the body is at rest or moving at constant velocity so the forces cancel, and dynamics, where they do not and the body accelerates. This lesson is entirely statics.

Two kinds of quantity appear throughout:

  • Scalars have magnitude only — mass, time, speed, temperature, energy, work.
  • Vectors have magnitude and direction, and must be added geometrically — force, moment, velocity, acceleration, displacement.

Confusing the two is the most expensive beginner's error in mechanics: two 5 kN forces do not necessarily make 10 kN.

SI units you are expected to use without thinking

QuantityUnitIn base units
Forcenewton, Nkg·m/s² — the force giving 1 kg an acceleration of 1 m/s²
Moment / torquenewton metre, N·mkg·m²/s²
Stress / pressurepascal, Pa = N/m²kg/(m·s²)

The idealizations that make the arithmetic possible

  1. Particle. A body whose size is ignored, so all forces acting on it pass through one point and no rotation is possible.
  2. Rigid body. A body whose deformation under load is small enough to be neglected while the external forces are found. Deflection and stress come later, in mechanics of materials — but the reactions are found on the rigid model first.
  3. Concentrated force. A load assumed to act at a single point, even though real contact is always spread over an area.
  4. Principle of transmissibility. A force may be slid along its own line of action without changing its external effect on a rigid body. Its internal effect — whether the member is pulled or pushed — does change, which is why the principle applies to rigid-body equilibrium only.
1.2

Forces, components and resultants

3 min

A force is the action of one body on another, fully described by four things: magnitude, direction, sense, and point of application. Forces that all lie in one plane are coplanar; forces whose lines of action meet at a single point are concurrent.

Because force is a vector, two forces combine by the parallelogram law — draw them from a common point, complete the parallelogram, and the diagonal is the resultant, the single force with the same effect as the set. The reverse operation, resolution, splits one force into components along chosen axes, and is what makes numerical work possible.

Rectangular components, two dimensions Fx = F cos θ    Fy = F sin θ    (θ measured from the x-axis)
F = √(Fx² + Fy²)    θ = tan⁻¹(Fy / Fx)
Rx = ΣFx    Ry = ΣFy    R = √(Rx² + Ry²)

To find the resultant of several coplanar forces, resolve every one of them into x and y components, add the components algebraically with a consistent sign convention (right and up positive), and recombine. This method of components replaces the drawn parallelogram with arithmetic, and it scales to any number of forces.

Worth remembering A force of 3 kN east and one of 4 kN north give a resultant of exactly 5 kN, at 53.1° north of east. Perpendicular forces are the one case where the Pythagorean shortcut applies directly — for any other angle use the components, or the cosine rule.
1.3

Moment of a force, and the couple

2 min

A force that does not pass through a point tends to rotate the body about it. That turning effect is the moment, and its magnitude is the force multiplied by the perpendicular distance from the point to the line of action of the force:

Moment about a point O MO = F × d    (d is the perpendicular distance — the moment arm)
Units: N·m   ·   Sign convention: choose one, usually anticlockwise positive

The word perpendicular carries all the weight. If a force of 60 N acts on a 0.3 m spanner at 45° to the handle, the moment is not 18 N·m but 60 × 0.3 × sin 45° = 12.7 N·m, because only the component perpendicular to the handle turns the nut. Varignon's theorem states this formally: the moment of a force about a point equals the sum of the moments of its components about that same point — which is usually the easier calculation.

Exam point A couple has no resultant force, yet it has a moment — and that moment is the same about any point in the plane. This is exactly why a body can satisfy ΣF = 0 and still spin: force equilibrium alone is not equilibrium.
1.4

Supports, reactions and the free-body diagram

2 min

The free-body diagram (FBD) is the single most important habit in this subject. You isolate the body from everything touching it, then draw every external force acting on it: applied loads, its own weight through the centre of gravity, and a reaction at each point where a support was removed. Internal forces between parts of the isolated body never appear — they occur in equal and opposite pairs and cancel.

What each support contributes is fixed by what motion it prevents. A support that prevents translation in a direction supplies a force in that direction; a support that prevents rotation supplies a moment.

Support (2-D)Motion preventedUnknowns
Roller / smooth surfaceTranslation normal to the surface only1 — a force perpendicular to the surface
Cable / linkTranslation along the cable (tension only)1 — a force along the cable
Smooth pin / hingeTranslation in both directions; rotation is free2 — force components in x and y
Fixed (built-in) endTranslation in both directions and rotation3 — two force components and a couple moment

One special case saves a great deal of work. A two-force member is a body loaded at only two points and carrying no other load or weight. For it to be in equilibrium, the two forces must be equal in magnitude, opposite in sense, and act along the straight line joining the two points — so the member is in pure tension or pure compression. Every link in a pin-jointed truss is a two-force member.

1.5

The conditions of equilibrium

1 min

A rigid body is in equilibrium when the force system acting on it has no resultant force and no resultant moment. In three dimensions that is six scalar equations; for the coplanar systems in this lesson it reduces to three:

Coplanar equilibrium — three independent equations ΣFx = 0   no net push left or right
ΣFy = 0   no net push up or down
ΣMA = 0   no net turning effect, about any point A

Three equations solve at most three unknowns. Compare the number of unknown reaction components with the number of available equations:

  • Statically determinate — unknowns equal the equations, so statics alone gives the answer.
  • Statically indeterminate — more unknowns than equations (a beam on three supports, or built in at both ends). The extra equations must come from the deformation of the body, which is the business of mechanics of materials.
  • Partially constrained — fewer unknowns than equations; the body cannot be held and will move.

Two useful consequences: a body under two forces is in equilibrium only if they are equal, opposite and collinear; a body under three non-parallel coplanar forces is in equilibrium only if their lines of action are concurrent — they meet at one point.

1.6

Worked example: reactions on a simply supported beam

2 min

A beam AB spans 6 m. It is pinned at A and rests on a roller at B. It carries a point load of 12 kN at 2 m from A and a uniformly distributed load of 3 kN/m over its whole length. Find the reactions.

  1. Replace the distributed load. A UDL of 3 kN/m over 6 m has a resultant of 3 × 6 = 18 kN, acting through the centroid of the load diagram — at mid-span, 3 m from A.
  2. Take moments about A (anticlockwise positive), which eliminates both components at A:
    By(6) − 12(2) − 18(3) = 0 → 6·By = 24 + 54 = 78 → By = 13 kN ↑
  3. Apply ΣFy = 0: Ay + 13 − 12 − 18 = 0 → Ay = 17 kN ↑
  4. Check with a second moment equation about B, which must also vanish:
    Ay(6) − 12(4) − 18(3) = 102 − 48 − 54 = 0 ✓
Method, in four moves Isolate the body → draw the FBD with every external force → choose a moment centre that kills the most unknowns → solve, then verify with an equation you did not use. The choice of moment centre is the whole skill: taking moments about a support with two unknown components removes both at a stroke.

Key terms to carry into the questions

Scalar / vector
Magnitude only, versus magnitude and direction added geometrically.
Resultant
The single force with the same external effect as a whole force system.
Transmissibility
A force may slide along its line of action on a rigid body without changing external effect.
Moment (M = F·d)
Turning effect; d is the perpendicular distance to the line of action.
Couple
Equal, opposite, parallel, non-collinear forces — zero resultant, constant moment F·d.
Free-body diagram
The body isolated, showing every external force and support reaction.
Two-force member
Loaded at two points only; forces are collinear, so it is in pure tension or compression.
Statically determinate
Unknown reactions can be found from the equilibrium equations alone.
